Terms

TermMeaning
obs classA kind of instrument: CTD/bottle casts (CTD_WOD), gliders (GLD_WOD), ice-tethered profilers (ITP), seal tags (MEOP), moorings (MRB_WOD), Argo floats (PFL), XBTs (XBT_WOD). Each is analyzed on its own.
reference, VrefWhat an observation V is compared with: the WOA23 climatology (“vs climatology”) or an ECCO model (“vs V4r6”, “vs V5r1”: each model’s monthly means at the profile’s model grid cell and month). “Compare V4r6 and V5r1” shows the two models side by side.
misfit ΔVV − Vref, in °C or psu.
σThe assumed error of an observation: how far from the reference it is expected to be. Not the instrument’s accuracy (much smaller); a derived map per depth (the Analyses page’s Terminology says where it comes from). In the explorer, “σ (assumed error)” maps it.
weight wEach observation’s own: w = γ·sf/σ², with γ the model grid cell’s area factor and sf the spatial scaling (both 0–1). An observation’s cost is w·(ΔV)²; ECCO’s cost is the sum over observations.
cost per datumThe average of (misfit / σ)² over many values (step by step, with an example: Cost per datum, step by step). 1 means one assumed error away; 4 means two. Analysis 09 is this against depth (the same as analysis 02’s red lines).
geodesic binOne of 10,242 cells of roughly equal area (about 220 km across) covering the globe.
depth levelOne of the 97 standard depths (2 m to 6000 m): 2–5 m apart in the top 100 m (23 levels), wider below (50 m around 1000 m, 100 m from 1100 to 2000 m), 200 m apart below 2000 m.
unsplit costw·(ΔV)² per value: the whole misfit.
split: anomaly and mean termECCO’s split of the cost per geodesic bin i and level k: anomaly term per value w·(ΔV − ΔV̄i,k)², the misfit after removing the bin’s long-term mean misfit ΔV̄i,k; and mean term per bin and level Ji,k = w̄·(ΔV̄i,k)², the steady offset itself, with w̄ = 1/max(s/n, s/9), s = mean(1/w) over the bin’s n values. (The “9” caps how much n independent values can sharpen the bin mean.)

kept / rejectedThe processing chain’s quality control (step 06) sets a value’s weight to 0 when it fails a test; such a value is rejected and enters no cost. Everything here uses kept values only.
platformThe instrument a profile comes from: an Argo float (WMO number), an ITP, a seal tag, a mooring; for the WOD ship classes, a ship or program code (coarser).

Cost per datum, step by step

Most of the site’s numbers are a cost per datum. It is the average of a simple score that every single observation gets. Here is how it is made, with a real example.

  1. A datum is one observation: one temperature (or salinity) value, at one depth, in one profile. More than one are data, so “# data” on the site is a count of these. A single Argo profile with 70 depth levels gives about 70 data for T and 70 for S.
  2. Its misfit is the reference minus the observation (the reference is the model or the climatology). Example: Argo float 6903082 at 2400 m on 29 March 2025 measured 2.810 °C; the model says 3.269 °C; the misfit is +0.46 °C.
  3. Divide by σ. σ is how big a misfit we expect for that value at that place and depth (a map made in advance: about 0.9 °C near the surface, about 0.1 °C deep down). Dividing asks “how many expected misfits is this?” Example: σ is 0.134 °C there, so 0.46 / 0.134 = 3.4: this value is 3.4 expected misfits off.
  4. Square it. 3.4² ≈ 11.8: that is this datum’s cost. Squaring makes every cost positive, whichever way the misfit points, and makes big misfits count much more than small ones:
    misfit, in units of σcost of that datum
    0.50.25
    11
    24
    3.411.8
  5. “Per datum” is the average of those costs over many data. In analysis 02, the value at 2400 m is the average cost of every datum the class has at 2400 m. So:
    • about 1: on average, the observations are about as far from the reference as σ predicts;
    • well above 1: a worse fit than σ predicts (or σ is too small there);
    • below 1: a better fit than σ predicts.

Why an average and not a total? A total grows with the amount of data: a depth or class with ten times more data would have about ten times the total even if it fit just as well. The average lets you compare depths, classes and years that have very different amounts of data. (The total is in analysis 04.)

One caution. It is an average of squares, so a few big misfits pull it up a lot. An average of 1 can mean every datum is about 1 σ off, or that eight data in nine fit almost perfectly and the ninth is 3 σ off (its cost is 9, and 9 spread over nine data is 1). The histogram, analysis 08, shows which.

Weights. “Per datum” on this site always means the plain score above, (misfit / σ)². The model’s own cost also multiplies each score by two more numbers between 0 and 1: γ (for the size of the model’s grid cell) and sf (so that crowded places don’t count more). Those are the final and nosf lines in analysis 02; the red true lines, and analysis 09, are the plain score.

The Analyses page

Choose the obs class, cost type and analysis with the tabs. Click a figure to see it full width. The side panel describes the figure; Terminology and Metadata open in it. Within a class, a figure uses the same axes in every cost type, so the tabs can be compared by eye.

Analysis 02 (cost per datum against depth) is where most investigations start. Below it, the “Investigate it” box opens the cost map explorer on the same class and cost type with a chosen cost part, variable and depth range, and runs the peak tool there (section The peak tool).

Simple / expert (top right): simple shows the everyday choices; expert adds the mean-bias diagnostic, depth bands and groups, flagged spikes, single year, the log color scale and regions. A link that uses an expert option opens in expert mode; your choice is remembered in this browser.

Rows of buttons (top): class, cost type, variable, cost part (unsplit, anomaly, mean term, number of data), the misfit, normalized misfit and σ (ref − obs, (ref − obs)/σ, the assumed error; not costs), depth (all, one level, 100 m bands, groups of levels, flagged spikes) and time (all years, or any range; the mean term has no years).

Maps: the global map (equal area) and the two polar maps; each bin colored by the selection. Hover a bin for its value; click a bin for its breakdown (the “Clicked bin” panel: per level, per year, and a link to its profiles).

Open it for the depth profile of the selection: the curve analysis 02 draws, for the current class, cost type, variable, part and years: for costs, at each level the mean over all its values (cost per datum); for the mean term, the mean over bins; for “# data”, the number of values. On the right, the number of bins per level (log scale).

Depth range (the shaded band on the profile): set it with the from/to menus or on the plot — drag the band’s top or bottom edge (the handles), drag inside to move it, drag across an empty part to draw a new one. Choosing a range does nothing by itself. It is used by:

  • also draw the curve over the same bins at every depth of the range: a second, blue curve over only the bins that have data at every level of the range (section Same bins). Attribution: from a cost peak to the profiles behind it

“Attribution” here means: for a feature in a cost curve (a peak at some depth), find how much of it is due to which data are counted at that depth, which bins carry it, and which platforms and profiles are in them. Every step is a fixed calculation on the saved results; nothing is fitted or judged. The page reports the numbers; reading them is up to you.

The steps, in the order the tools apply them:

  1. Find the peak in the chosen depth range.
  2. Same bins: is the jump a worse fit at that depth, or a different set of places being averaged there?
  3. Bin contributions: how much each bin adds to the curve at the peak.
  4. Clusters: group the largest contributors into areas, and name them.
  5. Region vs the rest: does the feature disappear without the area?
  6. Platforms: which instruments, profile by profile.
  7. Profiles: look at them.

A worked example (PFL, vs V4r6, T, mean term, 2000–2600 m) runs through all of them at the end.

1. The peak

Let ck be the curve at level k (what the depth profile draws). For costs it is the cost per datum over all values at the level: ck = Σi Ci,k / Σi Ni,k, with Ci,k the summed cost and Ni,k the number of values in bin i. For the mean term it is the mean over the Bk bins with data: ck = Σi Ji,k / Bk. These are exactly analysis 02’s curves.

The peak is the level p with the largest ck in the depth range. Its neighbors are the nearest levels with data above (p−) and below (p+); the excess is E = cp − (cp− + cp+)/2.

2. Same bins: a worse fit at that depth, or a different set of places?

The question. Each point on the cost-against-depth curve (one per depth) is an average over the places (geodesic bins) that have data at that depth. That list of places is not the same at every depth: most Argo floats stop at 2000 m, so below 2000 m far fewer places have data. When the curve jumps at one depth, there are two possible reasons:

  • A. A worse fit at that depth. In the same places, the misfit really is larger at that depth than above and below it.
  • B. A different set of places is averaged at that depth than at the depths above and below it. No place needs to fit worse: the average changes because some places drop out of the list (or join it), and the ones left are unusual.

An everyday version of B: a class’s average test score drops on Friday. Nobody did worse; half the class was absent, and the absent half were the strong students. Friday’s average is over a different set of students than Thursday’s.

The example (PFL, vs V4r6, T, mean term). The curve at 2400 m and the depths around it:

depthplaces with data at that depthaverage
2200 m1,1814.90
2400 m1,0465.57 (the jump)
2600 m4102.62

The three averages are over three different lists of places. At 2400 m, many of the places still on the list are in the subpolar North Atlantic, where the misfits are large; at 2600 m most of them have dropped out.

The test: average all three depths over one list: only the places with data at all three depths (410 of them). Then the list can’t change from depth to depth.

depththe same 410 placesaverage
2200 and 2600 m4102.79 (the two averaged)
2400 m4102.83
  • If the jump is still there over the same places, it is A: those places really fit worse at that depth.
  • If the jump disappears over the same places, it is B: it came from averaging over a different set of places.

Here the jump almost disappears (2.83 against 2.79), so the peak tool reports that 98 % of the jump comes from a different set of places being averaged at 2400 m (B), and 2 % from a worse fit at 2400 m (A).

That doesn’t make the peak wrong. The North Atlantic places really do fit badly; the jump just tells you about those places, not about the ocean as a whole at 2400 m.

The exact numbers (for checking): the jump is the peak’s value minus the average of the two depths around it, over all places (E = 5.57 − (4.90 + 2.62)/2 = 1.81) and over the same places (ES = 2.83 − 2.79 = 0.04). The share due to a different set of places is 1 − ES/E = 1 − 0.04/1.81 ≈ 98 %.

On the plot: the checkbox “also draw the curve over the same bins at every depth of the range” adds a blue curve over one list of places: those with data at every depth of the range. Where the red curve (all places) jumps and the blue one (the same places) doesn’t, the jump comes from a change in which places are averaged.

3. Bin contributions

Each bin’s part of the curve at the peak, chosen so the parts add up:

  • costs: bin i contributes its summed cost Ci,p; the curve is ΣC/ΣN, so the bin’s share of the level is Ci,p/ΣjCj,p.
  • mean term: bin i contributes Ji,p; share Ji,p/ΣjJj,p.

A share always comes with the bin’s share of the bins (or values): a region holding 4 % of the bins and 34 % of the term carries 8× its share. A large share from many bins is ordinary; a large share from few bins points to those few places.

4. Clusters and their names

The bins at the peak whose contribution is in the top 10 % are grouped into clusters: two high bins belong to the same cluster if their centers are within 600 km (chained, so a cluster can be long). Each cluster gets its center (the mean direction of its bins’ centers), its number of bins, its share of the bins and its share of the summed value at the peak. The largest cluster is outlined on the maps and becomes the region; click another row to switch.

What depth the maps show: the clusters are the high bins at the peak level, so the maps switch to that single level (the depth row at the top changes to it) and a note above the maps says so, with buttons to go back to the depth you had and to clear the outline. The outline is a set of bins, so it stays if you change the depth afterwards; only its meaning (“high at the peak level”) is tied to that level.

Names are descriptive only: the smallest Natural Earth sea, gulf or bay containing most of the cluster’s bin centers; otherwise a coarse hand-drawn box (e.g. “subpolar North Atlantic”, “Brazil Basin”, “Antarctic Circumpolar Current (south of Australia)”); otherwise the ocean. The coordinates are always shown next to the name.

A cluster holds only the high bins. To include their surroundings (to compare with nearby, well-fit bins), widen the region by dragging on the maps.

5. Region vs the rest

For any set of bins R (a cluster, or one you drag):

  • the depth profile draws the curve over R (purple), over the rest of the world (gray) and over all bins (light red), each formed as in step 1. If the region’s curve runs past the plot, it gets its own axis along the top. Levels where R has fewer than 5 bins are drawn pale. The decisive check: if the feature is gone from the gray curve, R accounts for it.
  • a table: R’s bins with data, its number of data, the curve over R and over the rest (per datum, or per bin for the mean term), and R’s share of the summed value, with how many times its share of bins that is;
  • per year: R (bars) against the rest (line); for the mean term, which has no years, reference − obs per year;
  • R’s ten largest bins (click one for its breakdown).

Caution: a region drawn around the darkest bins will always carry a large share. Judge it by its share relative to its share of bins and values, and by the rest-of-the-world curve, not by the share alone.

6. Platforms, profile by profile

The region panel lists the platforms in R with their profiles, years, mean reference − obs and share of the region’s value. The shares are computed profile by profile from every profile’s own values (the data viewer’s profile details), kept values at the selected levels only, true weights (w = 1/σ²):

  • unsplit cost: each value’s own (ΔV/σ)², summed per platform;
  • anomaly term: each value’s (ΔV − ΔV̄i,k)²/σ², with ΔV̄i,k the bin’s mean misfit over all years;
  • mean term: Ji,k = w̄·(ΔV̄)² is shared among the bin’s n values as w̄·ΔV̄·ΔVv/n for value v. The parts add up to J exactly (since ΣvΔVv = n·ΔV̄); a value pulling against its bin’s mean gets a negative part.

Shares are of the region’s total over its platforms. With a time window, values outside it are left out of the shares, but the bin means of the split use all years, as the analysis does.

Checked (2026-11-04): the mean term rebuilt this way from the profile details agrees with the analysis for every bin (PFL vs model, T, 2400 m: 1,046 bins, identical counts) to 0.14 % of its total; the difference is the details’ rounding to 0.001. If the details can’t be loaded, the table falls back to splitting each (bin, year)’s value among its platforms by their numbers of profiles there, and says so.

7. Profiles

“show profiles” opens the in situ data viewer with the platform’s profiles ringed, at the same class, reference and depth. Zoom in and click one for the profile panel (section The in situ data viewer). What to look for:

  • against its neighbors (gray): if the platform agrees with other platforms nearby and the reference doesn’t, the difference is in the reference (or real ocean variability the reference misses); if it alone is off, suspect the platform;
  • over time (second tab): a difference that persists from profile to profile is a water-mass (or model) difference; one that creeps is a likely sensor drift; a single bad profile stands alone;
  • against σ: how many assumed errors the misfit is; above about 3 is unusual;
  • rejected values (×): the chain already excluded them.

Worked example

PFL (Argo), vs V4r6, T, mean term, depth range 2000–2600 m (run 20261027_142247):

  1. Peak: 2400 m, 5.57 over 1,046 bins; neighbors 2200 m 4.90 (1,181 bins) and 2600 m 2.62 (410 bins).
  2. Same bins: over only the 410 places with data at all three depths, 2.83 against 2.79: the jump nearly disappears, so 98 % of it comes from a different set of places being averaged at 2400 m. Most floats stop at 2000 m; the few deeper ones are in a few regions.
  3. Clusters: the largest is the subpolar North Atlantic (56°N 38°W): 4.1 % of the bins, 33.8 % of the term.
  4. Region vs the rest: the region’s mean term per bin is 45.9 against 3.84 for the rest of the world.
  5. Platforms: four floats deployed there since 2021 (6903082, 6903871, 6903085, 1902621) carry about 40 % of the region’s term, each with model − obs about +0.37 to +0.40 °C.
  6. Profiles: float 6903082 at 2400 m in March 2025: obs 2.810 °C, model 3.269 °C (−0.46 °C, 3.4σ); it agrees with the other floats nearby; the cold layer at 2000–2500 m persists over its life.

Reading (not a test the tools run): the model is warmer than the floats in the subpolar North Atlantic at 2000–2500 m; the global mean term peaks at 2400 m because, at that depth, the North Atlantic is a much bigger share of the places with data. The model values are monthly means, not the model’s values at each profile’s time.

Cautions

  • Few bins or values make any number noisy: look at the counts shown next to everything (pale = under 5 bins).
  • Regions drawn after looking always look important; compare shares with shares of bins and values.
  • The model costs are approximate: monthly-mean model fields, so sub-monthly variability counts as misfit; no model values after December 2025.
  • Precision: the explorer stores 3 significant digits per value; the profile details 0.001 (σ 0.0001).
  • Platform IDs for the WOD ship classes are ship or program codes, not single instruments.
